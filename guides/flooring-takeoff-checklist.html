Flooring takeoff checklist

A working list to run against a real set. Flooring looks like the simplest trade to count and isn't — the area is easy, and almost everything that costs money on a flooring job lives at the edges of it.

Guide · Published 19 September 2026 · SharpBid Estimating

Where do you start a flooring takeoff?

By reading the finish schedule and the plan tags against each other, room by room, before measuring anything. On most sets the two disagree somewhere — and whichever one you priced, the other is the one that gets built.

Finish schedules and finish plans are produced at different times and revised at different times. Rooms that changed late are exactly the ones where the schedule still says one thing and the plan tag says another. Reconciling them first turns a future change order into an RFI you send during the bid period.

  • List every finish code from the schedule with its full specification before measuring.
  • Tie every room to a code, from both the schedule and the plan tag.
  • Record every disagreement with the room number and both readings. That list goes in your bid.
  • Check for rooms with no finish called up at all — they exist on most sets, usually service spaces.

How should areas be measured?

Room by room, to the finished face of wall, with one deduction rule applied everywhere. Consistency matters more than which rule you pick, because your unit rates need to mean the same thing on every line.

  • Measure to the face of finish, not to structure or gridline.
  • Decide the deduction rule once — casework, millwork bases, columns, floor-mounted equipment — and write it down.
  • Keep rooms separate rather than summing to a floor total. You'll need them again when the schedule changes.
  • Separate levels, and on repetitive residential separate suites, corridors and amenity.

Roll goods and modular tile are different problems

Broadloom and sheet vinyl are governed by roll width and seam layout, not just area. Carpet tile and LVT are area plus pattern. Treating them identically is how material orders come up short.

  • Keep roll goods in their own section, with room dimensions preserved so a cut plan can be worked out.
  • Note seam-sensitive areas — corridors, long open spans, anywhere the seam layout drives the order.
  • Record roll width if the spec or the supplier fixes it.
  • Welded seams in sheet vinyl are lineal footage with labour attached, common in institutional work.

Have you recorded pattern and direction?

Pattern changes what you buy over the same square footage, and it's set in the spec or on the finish plan rather than being an installer's choice.

  • Carpet tile lay: quarter-turn, monolithic, ashlar, brick.
  • Plank direction for LVT and LVP, and whether it changes between spaces.
  • Herringbone, chevron and any feature pattern, which carry materially different cutting.
  • Tile layout and setting-out points, where the drawings dictate them.
  • Directional or dye-lot-sensitive material, which affects how it must be ordered.

Transitions, base and the edges where the money is

The single most common flooring undercount: transitions counted per door leaf. Transitions happen wherever two finishes meet, and on an open plan a lot of those junctions have no door anywhere near them.

  • Count transitions by condition, not by door — open-plan boundaries, corridor edges, thresholds without doors.
  • Identify the profile for each condition: reducer, T-moulding, carpet-to-tile, metal edge, threshold.
  • Base off the room perimeter net of openings, by finish type and height.
  • Base switches where the floor finish switches — tile base in wet areas, rubber elsewhere. Match them deliberately.
  • Coved base is a different product and a different rate from straight base.

Stair components

Stairs are counted as components, never as "a stair". Each piece carries its own material and its own install rate.

  • Treads and risers counted each, by material.
  • Nosings in lineal feet, including any tactile or contrasting insert.
  • Stringers in lineal feet where they're finished.
  • Landings in area, with their own finish and base.

Moisture mitigation, levelling and the substrate

This is where flooring bids are won and lost, and none of it is on the finish schedule. It's in the Division 09 specification and the existing-conditions notes.

  • Find the moisture testing requirement and read what it triggers. "Mitigation where relative humidity exceeds the manufacturer's limit" is an open allowance with your name on it.
  • Quantify the slab areas it could apply to — slab-on-grade first, then suspended slabs — and qualify a square footage rather than carrying the whole floor plate.
  • Floor preparation: levelling, grinding, patching. On a renovation over an existing slab this is often the largest single variable in the bid.
  • Flatness tolerance, which the spec may set higher than the concrete sub was required to deliver — that gap is yours unless you qualify it.
  • Crack isolation and uncoupling membrane under tile.
  • Waterproofing membrane in wet areas, including the upturns at walls and curbs from the details, not just the flat area.
  • Acoustic underlay by assembly, which in multifamily is driven by the acoustic report rather than the finish schedule.

How should waste be handled?

Stated, never buried. Measured quantities should be net, with waste listed separately per material so you can apply your own figures.

The right allowance is not a universal number and anyone quoting you one without seeing the job is guessing. It depends on the material and how it cuts, the pattern and direction, room shapes and how much cutting the plan forces, roll width and seam layout for roll goods, dye lot and attic stock requirements in the spec, and your own crews' habits. Two estimators can be equally competent and land in different places on the same job — which is exactly why the number belongs in the open where you can change it.

  • Keep measured quantities net.
  • State waste per material on the assumptions page, as its own column or line.
  • Check the spec for attic stock, which is extra material on top of waste and is easy to miss entirely.
  • Never accept a takeoff with waste baked in. If you can't see the allowance, you can't check it and you can't change it.

The items that may not be yours

  • Recessed entrance matting. Often Division 12, with the frame supplied by someone else and the recess formed by the concrete trade. Name the division before quoting.
  • Demolition of existing finishes on tenant improvement work — sometimes yours, sometimes the general contractor's.
  • Sealers and finishes on polished concrete, which may sit with the concrete trade.
  • Protection of finished floors after installation, which the spec sometimes assigns and sometimes doesn't.

Before you submit

  • Does every room trace to a finish code and a measured area?
  • Is the schedule-versus-plan conflict list in the bid?
  • Is the moisture mitigation position stated as a quantity, not left open?
  • Is waste visible and stated per material?
  • Is the deduction rule written down and applied everywhere?
  • Are exclusions itemized rather than covered by a blanket qualification?
